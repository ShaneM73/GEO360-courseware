Why three spheres can still leave two positions

After S1 and S2 intersect, their common solution is a circle. Intersecting that circle with the third sphere normally produces two points, P and P′.

Key geometry: the plane through S1–S3 is a mirror plane. P and P′ are symmetric with respect to that plane.

Both points satisfy the same three ranges

|S1P| = |S1P′| = l₁
|S2P| = |S2P′| = l₂
|S3P| = |S3P′| = l₃

Reflection across the S1–S3 plane does not change the distance to any point lying in that plane. Therefore all three measured ranges are identical for P and P′.

Real Earth vs. virtual Earth

The blue Earth is placed on the side containing the physically plausible receiver P. The purple “virtual Earth” is its mirror image across the satellite plane and contains P′ in the corresponding symmetric position.

Important: three perfect ranges alone cannot distinguish these two mirror solutions. A fourth independent range, or an external constraint such as “the receiver must be near the real Earth,” removes the ambiguity.

What to point out in class

Rotate the model until the satellite triangle is nearly edge-on. The two Earths and the two candidate points will appear on opposite sides of the triangle plane, while each pair of equal-distance rays remains symmetric.

GPS Trilateration, Nonlinear Equations, and Receiver Clock Bias

This note separates the ideal geometric trilateration problem from the real GPS pseudorange estimation problem. The distinction matters: ordinary circle/sphere geometry explains why additional range surfaces remove geometric ambiguity, while real GPS introduces receiver clock bias and solves a nonlinear estimation problem by local linearization and iteration.

1. Two related—but different—problems

Do not apply the linear “n equations for n unknowns” rule blindly. Nonlinear systems can have zero, one, two, or many solutions. Equation counting tells us the minimum number of independent constraints needed locally; uniqueness also depends on geometry and the rank of the Jacobian.

2. Ideal 2D trilateration: b = 0

For a known satellite/beacon location (xi, yi) and an exact geometric range ri, the receiver lies on a circle:

ri2=(x−xi)2+(y−yi)2

With only two circles, the two equations are nonlinear and normally have two common solutions. This is exactly what the 2D animation highlights.

Exact linearization by subtraction—no Taylor expansion required

For ideal squared-range equations, subtract circle 1 from circle i. The common x² and y² terms cancel exactly:

2(xi−x1)x + 2(yi−y1)y = r12−ri2 +xi2−x12 +yi2−y12

Three circles provide two independent difference equations (2−1 and 3−1), producing a 2×2 linear system for x and y. Thus the third range removes the two-point ambiguity. This is an exact algebraic cancellation, not a Taylor approximation.

3. Ideal 3D trilateration: b = 0

In three dimensions, one exact range defines a sphere:

ri2= (x−xi)2+ (y−yi)2+ (z−zi)2
  • Sphere 1 ∩ Sphere 2 → generally a circle.
  • That circle ∩ Sphere 3 → generally two points.
  • Sphere 4 normally passes through only one candidate → unique geometric solution.

Again, if b = 0 and the ranges are exact, subtracting squared sphere equations cancels x²+y²+z² exactly. Four spheres give three independent linear difference equations for x, y, and z. Taylor expansion is not necessary for this idealized case.

4. What receiver clock bias actually means

A GPS satellite transmits a precisely time-tagged signal. The receiver estimates signal travel time by comparing the transmitted time with its own reception time. Satellite clocks are atomic clocks; the inexpensive receiver clock is not perfectly synchronized with GPS time.

b=c·δtr
δtᵣ = receiver clock offset in seconds; c = speed of light; b = range-equivalent clock bias in meters.
δtr
Receiver clock offset. Unit: seconds (commonly μs or ns for discussion).
c
Speed of light ≈ 299,792,458 m/s.
b = cδtr
Equivalent distance error. Unit: meters or kilometers.
Example: a +1 μs receiver clock error corresponds to approximately +299.8 m of pseudorange bias. A displayed b = +0.80 km corresponds to δtr ≈ +2.67 μs.

This is why the main-page slider now uses μs for the actual clock offset and simultaneously reports the equivalent distance bias b. The slider is not applied to the ideal geometry animation, because doing so would prevent the highlighted candidate from exactly overlapping the known red teaching point.

5. Real GPS pseudorange equations

After applying satellite-clock and atmospheric corrections, a simplified pseudorange model is

ρi= (x−xi)2+ (y−yi)2+ (z−zi)2 +b+εi

Define the geometric distance Ri(x,y,z) as the square-root term. The unknown state is

u = [ x y z b ]ᵀ

With four satellites, we have four nonlinear pseudorange equations for four state parameters. But that counting statement alone does not prove a unique solution. The equations must be locally independent, which is tested through the Jacobian matrix.

6. Taylor expansion: converting the nonlinear GPS model into a local linear system

Choose an approximate receiver state u₀ = [x₀, y₀, z₀, b₀]ᵀ and seek a correction Δu. For each satellite:

Fi(u0+Δu) ≈ Fi(u0) + JiΔu

Using the predicted pseudorange \hat{ρ}i,0 = Ri,0 + b₀, define the observed-minus-computed residual

vi=ρi−(Ri,0+b0)

The first-order Taylor approximation gives

vi≈ x0−xiRi,0Δx + y0−yiRi,0Δy + z0−ziRi,0Δz +Δb

This is a local linear model. After solving for Δu, update the approximate state and repeat until the correction becomes sufficiently small.

7. Jacobian (design) matrix J

For m satellites, stack the linearized equations:

v ≈ J Δu ⎡ (x₀-x₁)/R₁ (y₀-y₁)/R₁ (z₀-z₁)/R₁ 1 ⎤ ⎢ (x₀-x₂)/R₂ (y₀-y₂)/R₂ (z₀-z₂)/R₂ 1 ⎥ J = ⎢ ⋮ ⋮ ⋮ ⋮ ⎥ ⎣ (x₀-xₘ)/Rₘ (y₀-yₘ)/Rₘ (z₀-zₘ)/Rₘ 1 ⎦ Δu = [ Δx Δy Δz Δb ]ᵀ

The first three columns describe satellite line-of-sight geometry; the final column is 1 because ∂(R+b)/∂b = 1.

Local solvability condition: for 3D GPS with clock bias, J must have column rank 4. Four rows are the minimum possible, but four satellites with poor geometry can make J nearly singular or ill-conditioned. This is the mathematical origin of geometry-dependent precision and DOP.

8. Iterative solution

Exactly four independent satellites

J Δu = v → Δu = J⁻¹v (only if J is nonsingular)

More than four satellites

Operational GNSS usually tracks more than four satellites, giving an overdetermined system. The correction is obtained by least squares:

Δu = (Jᵀ W J)⁻¹ Jᵀ W v

W is a weight matrix that can reflect measurement quality. In numerical software, QR or SVD methods are generally preferred to explicitly forming the matrix inverse.

  1. Choose initial state u₀.
  2. Compute geometric ranges Ri,0 and predicted pseudoranges.
  3. Form residual vector v and Jacobian J.
  4. Solve for Δu.
  5. Update u ← u + Δu.
  6. Repeat until position and clock corrections are below chosen tolerances.

10. Key conclusions

  • Ideal 2D: two circles usually leave two points; a third circle resolves the ambiguity.
  • Ideal 3D: two spheres form an intersection circle; a third sphere reduces it to two points; a fourth resolves the ambiguity.
  • No Taylor expansion is needed to linearize the ideal b=0 squared-range equations by subtraction—the quadratic terms cancel exactly.
  • Real GPS is different: pseudorange contains receiver clock bias b = cδtr, so the state is [x,y,z,b]ᵀ.
  • The real GPS equations are nonlinear. Taylor expansion produces a local linear correction system v ≈ JΔu.
  • “Four equations for four unknowns” is only a minimum counting argument. A valid local solution requires rank(J)=4, and good accuracy requires well-conditioned satellite geometry.
  • With more than four satellites, GNSS uses an overdetermined least-squares solution, normally with measurement weighting and repeated iterations.
