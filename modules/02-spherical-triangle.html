Why this matters for aerial photos

In the class KML case (λ = 90°) all three corners are right angles, yet the figure is still a triangle. "Straight lines" on a sphere are great circles, and they bend away from Euclidean rules.

The excess E grows with the triangle's area. A single aerial photo footprint (a few km) has an excess of a tiny fraction of an arc-second, so the flat-plane similar-triangle geometry used in the next modules is an excellent approximation.

Over regions, states, or continents, curvature can no longer be ignored — that is the job of datums and map projections.
1. Sum > 180°Any spherical triangle's angles total more than 180°. The surplus is the spherical excess E.
2. Excess ∝ areaArea = E (in radians) × R². Halve the triangle's area and the excess halves too.
3. Small = flatShrink λ toward 0° and watch the sum approach 180° — but note vertex A stays on the pole. Truly small triangles behave Euclidean.
